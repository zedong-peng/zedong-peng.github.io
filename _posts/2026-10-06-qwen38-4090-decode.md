Qwen3.8-27B decodes at 322 tokens per second on one RTX 4090, serving one user. On the same card and benchmark, vLLM decodes 200 tokens per second and llama.cpp 102. With the official weights left untouched, our engine reaches 245.

The text does not change: every token is the model's own greedy choice. The weights behind the last number are our own 4-bit build, and they are closer to the full-precision model than the official 4-bit release.

## Memory bandwidth

With one user, every forward pass reads every weight once. At about 4 bits per weight the model is 13 to 14 GB, and the RTX 4090 reads its memory at close to 1 TB per second. That caps ordinary decoding, one token per pass, near 70 tokens per second. We measured 46 in llama.cpp and 54 in the engine we started from.

Speculative decoding changes the unit of work. A small drafter model proposes several tokens. The target model scores all of them in one pass, which still reads the weights only once, keeps the ones that match its own greedy choices, and adds one token of its own. The drafter can change the speed but never the output.

Speed is then the tokens a round accepts divided by the time a round takes, and a round's time is mostly the bytes it reads divided by the card's bandwidth. That leaves three things to work on: more tokens per round, less time in which memory sits idle, and fewer bytes.

## A tree of drafts

The engine we started from drafts a chain of 7 tokens: if the third is wrong, the rest are wasted. We draft a tree of 15 candidates instead, grown from the drafter's most likely tokens at each position, and the target verifies the whole tree in one pass. Where the chain breaks, a side branch often continues.

The tree accepts 5.2 tokens per round where the chain accepted 4.0. Trees were already in the upstream project's code; we merged them into the port for this card. Each round now verifies 16 positions instead of 8, and at first that made rounds longer. Retuning the matrix kernels for this GPU, which now stream 930 to 1,000 GB per second, took a profiled round from 25.3 to 22.9 ms, and on the benchmark a round takes what the chain's did.

A wider tree does not pay here. A tree of 32 accepts about a tenth more tokens, but its activations no longer fit the kernels' 16-column tiles, and the matrix kernels cost 12 to 39 percent more.

## Shorter rounds

A round is almost all weight reading. In that profiled round of 22.9 ms, 20.4 ms goes to streaming weights. The rest goes to hundreds of small kernels, and while they run, memory sits idle.

Three kinds of change took the benchmark round from 22.6 to 21.1 ms, still on the official weights:

- **A cheaper output layer.** The output layer scores 248,320 tokens at every position. It is first read through a 3-bit copy that keeps 32 candidates per position, and only those are scored exactly. The output was identical on all 480 prompts.
- **Idle time put to use.** While a small kernel runs, a few extra threads in the same launch pull the first megabytes of the next matrix into the GPU's cache, so the next kernel starts warm. The results are bit-identical, because the extra threads only load data.
- **Cheaper small kernels.** Attention over the tree was rewritten and takes 7 µs instead of 17. Compiling the engine as one program let the largest kernel use fewer registers, so three copies of it fit on each processor instead of two.

That leaves a round that is almost all streaming, and one large lever: fewer bytes.

## Fewer bytes

A smaller model that is worse than the official one would not count, so we fixed the bar first. The reference is the full-precision model. We score 46,059 next-token predictions, half English prose and half Python source, on three numbers: the divergence of the output distribution from the reference, how often the top token agrees, and perplexity. A change ships only if every number, on prose and on code, is at least as good as the official 4-bit release.

The official release rounds each weight to the nearest 4-bit or 5-bit value. Choosing the rounding with GPTQ, which corrects each weight's error with the weights after it, cuts the divergence by more than a third at the same size. We spend that headroom on bytes: every matrix goes to 4 bits, the feed-forward matrices of the early layers, where a bit costs the least quality, go to 3, and the quantization scales are then tuned end to end against the full-precision model's outputs.

With these weights a round takes 16.8 ms instead of 21.1.

Quality turned out to be a speed variable too. A noisier target agrees less often with the drafter, which was trained on the full-precision model: a naive all-4-bit build read fewer bytes but accepted fewer tokens per round, and lost most of what it had saved. For the same reason we fine-tuned the drafter on text generated by the new target, which was worth another 2.3% in tokens per second.

## Small effects

Late in the project most gains were under one percent of round time, and speculative decoding makes effects that small hard to see. A change that is not bit-exact flips a few near-tied tokens; from the first flip on the text differs, and different text is accepted at a different rate. A change we knew to do nothing measured as 0.44% more tokens per round.

Three practices made such effects measurable. The engine can be forced to follow a reference text, so every configuration walks the same 480 outputs. Two configurations alternate on one GPU in the order A B B A B A A B, which cancels the drift of a warming card. And each prompt is compared with itself across runs, which removes the difference between easy and hard prompts. The last change we adopted was measured this way, at 0.16% faster.

## Limitations

- One user and greedy decoding only. Sampling needs a different path through the output layer.
- One kind of card. Most kernel decisions are specific to the RTX 4090.
- Most benchmark prompts are short. On prompts of 11,000 and 32,000 tokens, rounds take 18 to 20 ms and accept fewer tokens, and the gain over the engine we started from falls from 1.8 times to 1.55.
- Our build keeps extra copies of some weights for decoding, about 4 GB. It starts with a context of 57,344 tokens but not 65,536, which the official weights on the same engine still fit.
- The quality bar covers English prose and Python.
- The other engines ran their published recipes without further tuning, and we did not score their 4-bit builds with our quality harness.

## Code and data

The engine is a series of 53 patches on Cinference, in jram4's port to this GPU. The [code](https://github.com/zedong-peng/qwen38-4090-decode) includes the measurement tools, and [REPRODUCE.md](https://github.com/zedong-peng/qwen38-4090-decode/blob/master/REPRODUCE.md) goes from a fresh clone to each number here, for our engine and for the vLLM and llama.cpp recipes. The [weights](https://huggingface.co/zedongpeng/Qwen3.8-27B-NInfer-4090) are on Hugging Face.

This work builds on NInfer (Neroued), Cinference (satellitedown) and jram4's port of it, on the DFlash2 drafter (z-lab), on Spec-Bench (Xia et al.), and on the community llama.cpp and vLLM recipes for this model.

An AI coding agent, Claude Code, ran this project on the machine for four days: it designed and ran the experiments, wrote the kernels and kept the research log, while I set the goals, the constraints and the quality bar. Every number here comes from a logged run.
